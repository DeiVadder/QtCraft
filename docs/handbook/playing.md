## QtCraft

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-basics"></a>Getting started

**What to do**

QtCraft is a world of blocks. Collect them, build with them, craft tools, smelt metal and stay alive: creatures come out at night and hunger grows while you work.

**The first day**

- Break a few logs of a tree with your hand (hold the left mouse button).
- Open the inventory (E) and turn the logs into planks, then planks into sticks and a crafting table.
- Place the crafting table and use it (right mouse button) to make a wooden pickaxe.
- Mine stone for a stone pickaxe and look for coal: coal and a stick make torches.
- Build a shelter before the night; a day lasts 600 s.

**This handbook**

Press H / F1 at any time. Every block, item, creature and biome has a page with its values, its recipes and links to where it comes from and what it is used for. In the inventory, the furnace and chests, point at an item and press H / F1 or click the small ? on it.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-controls"></a>Controls

**Keys and mouse**

- **Mouse**: look around (click into the window first), or **↑ ← ↓ →**
- **W A S D**: walk, **Space**: jump or swim up, **Shift**: sneak, **Ctrl**: sprint
- **Left mouse** or **F**: mine, attack a creature
- **Right mouse** or **R**: place, use, eat
- **Middle mouse** or **B**: pick the block you look at
- **Q**: throw one item, **Ctrl+Q**: the whole stack
- **1–9**, mouse wheel: choose the hotbar slot; hold **G** for the hotbar wheel and choose a slot by moving the mouse
- **E**: inventory, **T / Enter**: chat, **/**: command, **V / F5**: camera view, **H / F1**: handbook, **I / F3**: debug display (off, compact, full), **O / F4**: debug options, **Esc**: menu; in a single-player world it pauses the game until you return to it, unless other players are connected (the handbook and settings opened from the menu keep the pause)
- Every key except Esc can be changed under Settings → Controls.

**Mac keyboards**

On a Mac the keys F1–F12 control the brightness, Mission Control, the search and other system features, so the game does not receive them. Every action therefore has a letter key, for example **V** for the camera view and **H** for the handbook pages. To use the function keys in the game, hold fn or switch on “Use F1, F2, etc. keys as standard function keys” in the system settings (Keyboard → Keyboard Shortcuts → Function Keys). No key of the game uses Cmd, and keys pressed with Cmd do nothing in the game, because macOS keeps Cmd+Q, Cmd+H, Cmd+W, Cmd+Space and Cmd+Tab for itself: sprint is Option on a Mac, and Control+Q throws the whole stack. If you put an action on Control, note that Control with the arrow keys or Space switches desktops or the input source. Option+E, U, N and I are dead keys only while typing text; in the world they work as sprint plus that key.

**Gamepad**

A gamepad (Xbox, PlayStation, Switch Pro and most others) steers the player while the game window has the focus, together with keyboard and mouse: a pushed stick moves you, otherwise the keys do. Buttons are named by their place, so the lower face button is A on an Xbox pad, Cross on a PlayStation pad and B on a Switch pad.
- **Left stick**: walk; the further you push it, the faster you walk
- **Right stick**: look around
- **Right trigger**: mine, attack a creature; **left trigger**: place, use, eat
- **Lower face button**: jump or swim up; press it twice quickly in creative mode to fly or land
- **Press the left stick**: sprint while walking forward
- **Press the right stick**: sneak on or off; while flying, hold it to descend
- **Left and right shoulder buttons**: previous and next hotbar slot
- **Right face button**: drop one item; hold it to drop the stack
- **Left face button**: take the block you look at into your hand: from your inventory, in creative mode from the catalogue as well
- **Upper face button**: inventory; **Start**: pause menu; **Back**: hold to see the players; while holding it, the upper face button takes a screenshot and the left one hides the HUD
- **D-pad**: up switches the camera view, down opens the chat, right the handbook; hold left for the hotbar wheel: point at a slot with the right stick and let go to take it

In the menus, the inventory and every other screen the d-pad or the left stick moves a golden ring from button to button and slot to slot; the lower face button presses or picks up and puts down, the right one goes back, the left one puts down a single item, the upper one moves a stack across, the shoulder buttons switch tabs and the right stick scrolls. On a slider or a number field, left and right change the value. A text field opens a keyboard on the screen (Shift gives each key its second character, for example ! and ? or capitals): the left face button deletes, the upper one types a space, Start finishes. As soon as you use the mouse or the keyboard again, the ring goes away.

The hints at the bottom show the buttons as your controller labels them: letters for Xbox and Switch pads, cross, circle, square and triangle for PlayStation pads, and for other pads a small diamond with the place of the button lit. When a controller connects or disconnects, a short notice says so; if the controller you play with disconnects in the world, the pause menu opens (not for a controller that only lies connected while you play with keyboard and mouse). With several controllers, the one you last pressed a button on is used.

**Settings → Controller** sets look speed, inverted looking, the dead zone and response curve of the sticks, the dead zone of the triggers and how far you pull them before they act, and vibration (the controller you play with rumbles when you are hurt, if it can). Each controller model can have its own settings (*Own settings*); otherwise it uses the settings for all controllers. The same controller over USB and over Bluetooth counts as two models. On Switch pads, too, the lower button (B) confirms and the right one (A) goes back, as the buttons sit on every other pad. Under *Buttons*, choose *Change* and press the new button on the controller; a button another action had is swapped. A key or a click cancels. In menus the buttons keep their places. *Radial menu only* hides the hotbar but for the slot in your hand: the hotbar wheel shows all slots.

**Moving**

You walk at 4.3 m/s, sprint at 5.6 m/s and sneak at 1.3 m/s; sneaking keeps you from falling off edges. To sprint, press **Ctrl** while walking forward or tap **W** twice; the sprint lasts as long as you keep walking forward. The view widens a little while you sprint (Settings → FOV effects). Steps up to 0.5 m are climbed without jumping. In water you swim at 2.2 m/s.

**Screens**

In the inventory, furnace and chest screens a left click picks a stack up and puts it down (merging or swapping), a right click puts down a single item. The list on the right of the inventory crafts recipes directly.

**Interface size**

**Settings → General → Interface size** makes the HUD and every screen larger or smaller: 75 % to 200 % in steps of 25, or *Auto*, which picks the size from the window (a window of 1920 × 1080 gives 150 %, 2560 × 1440 gives 200 %). A screen never grows beyond the window: in a small window the screens stay as large as fits, and the number next to the choice shows the size in use; the HUD needs less room and may grow further. Screen readers find every button, slider, list and slot by its name (a slot by its item and count).

**Display**

**Settings → Display** sets how the window shows: *Window*, *Borderless fullscreen* (a window without frame covering the screen) or *Fullscreen*, and with several monitors the screen it uses; the game remembers the window's place. **F11** switches fullscreen on and back to the mode before. *VSync* waits for the monitor and avoids tearing; it applies after a restart. The *frame rate limit* (30 to 144 frames a second, or unlimited) keeps the graphics card cooler and quieter; VSync without a limit gives the smoothest picture. A second limit, 30 unless you change it, applies to the world behind the pause menu, to the character previews and while another window has the focus. The compact debug display names the limit in force.

**Settings → Graphics → Render mode** chooses the look of the world. *Classic* draws it as it always was. *Realistic* draws it with a wider range of brightness: mist gathers in valleys and low land and thins out above them, light sources, lava and the sun glow softly into their surroundings, and the brightest light rolls off gently instead of clipping. Caves stay clear of the mist. The sun casts shadows: trees, houses, creatures and cliffs darken what lies behind them, light falls through the gaps of leaves, the shadows grow long towards evening, and on clear nights the moon casts faint ones; the shaded side turns slightly blue, sunlight at dusk warm. Corners, the ground under creatures and the shade under overhangs darken softly, and a torch or lantern in your hand lights the area around you as you walk; so do lights other players carry and glowing fireflies. This light is only drawn: the light level of the world stays the same. *Realistic quality* chooses how much the graphics card spends: *Low* without the glow and the soft shade, with short shadows and a few moving lights, *Medium* and *High* with a wider glow, finer shade, sharper and longer shadows and more moving lights. *Exposure* makes the realistic picture darker or brighter. The realistic mode costs more graphics power than the classic one.

**Photo mode**

**P / F6** hides the HUD: the crosshair, the hotbar, the hearts, the chat lines, the labels and the outline of the block you look at, and the hand as well unless you switch that off under Settings → General. Press it again to show everything. **K / F2** saves a picture of the window as a PNG file in the folder *screenshots* next to your worlds, named after the date and time; a short notice tells you its name. On a gamepad hold **Back** and press the upper face button for a screenshot or the left face button to hide the HUD; the player list shows meanwhile, and the two buttons do nothing else.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-mining"></a>Mining

**Breaking blocks**

Hold the left mouse button on a block within 5 m. Each hardness point takes 1.5 s by hand; the right tool divides that time by its speed. Broken blocks drop their items, which you pick up by walking over them.

**Tools and levels**

Stone and ores need a pickaxe of a certain level: wood is level 1, stone 2, iron 3, diamond 4. With a weaker tool or none, mining takes 5 s per hardness point and drops nothing. Tools wear out with every block.

**Ores and raw metal**

Iron, gold and copper ore do not drop themselves but **raw metal**: raw iron, raw gold and two to five pieces of raw copper. A furnace smelts each piece into an ingot. Nine pieces of raw metal pack into a block of raw metal for storage, which crafts back into nine. Copper ore is common from deep below up into the mountains, iron lies below the middle heights, gold only deep down. Coal, diamonds, lapis lazuli, emeralds and Signal Dust drop their gems and dust directly.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-building"></a>Building

**Placing blocks**

Use the held block on a face (right mouse button) to place it next to that face. Stairs and furnaces turn by your view, slabs and stairs go high or low by where you click, torches and ladders hang on the face you click. Tall grass is replaced by what you place into it.

**Support**

Torches, ladders, lamps, doors and plants need something to stand or hang on. When it goes, they break and drop. Sand and gravel fall when nothing is below them.

**Building parts**

Many materials come as stairs, slabs, walls, fences and gates. Fences, walls and panes connect to their neighbours. Doors, trapdoors and gates open and close when used; sneak to place a block against them instead.

**Stair corners**

Stairs next to stairs form corners by themselves, as roofs need them: a stair turned across in front of another (on the side it climbs to) makes that one an outer corner with a quarter step, a stair turned across behind it an inner corner with three quarters. Stairs in a straight row stay straight, and only stairs of the same half (both upright or both upside down) join. Every corner keeps changing with its neighbours.

**Vertical slabs**

Every material with slabs also has vertical slabs: three slabs on top of each other in the crafting grid give three, one vertical slab gives a slab back. A vertical slab leans against the face you click; placed on a floor or ceiling it stands on the far side of the cell as you look. A second vertical slab facing the other way, or one placed on the open side, fills the cell; broken, it drops both.

**Lying logs and stripped wood**

Logs, wood, stripped logs and bone blocks lie along the face you click: on a top or bottom face they stand upright, on a side face they lie towards it, with the rings at their ends. Use an axe on a log or on wood to strip its bark; it keeps lying the way it did and nothing drops.

**Signs**

A sign stands on the floor, turned towards you in sixteen steps, or hangs on the side of a block. When you place it, a screen opens for its text: four lines of up to 15 characters each. Everyone near it can read the text; use the sign again to change it, and sneak to place a block against it instead. Use a dye on a sign with text to colour the text in that dye's colour (one dye; none in creative mode, and none if the text already has that colour); the text starts black. Six planks over a stick make three signs.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-crafting"></a>Crafting

**The crafting grid**

The inventory has a 2 × 2 grid; a crafting table within 5 m opens 3 × 3. Lay out the ingredients as the recipe shows (shaped recipes may sit anywhere in the grid and be mirrored, shapeless ones in any order) and take the result. Leaving the screen puts the grid back into the inventory.

**Recipe list and groups**

The inventory lists what you can make right now; a click crafts it. Some recipes ask for a group such as any planks or coal or charcoal: every item of the group fits.

**The stonecutter**

A stonecutter cuts one stone block at a time into the shapes of its family without loss: two slabs or two vertical slabs, one stair or one wall per block, and the cut and chiseled variants (stone into stone bricks and chiseled stone bricks, sandstone into cut and carved sandstone, and on into their shapes). Use it and put stone into the slot on the left; the middle shows what it becomes. Choose a result and take it from the slot on the right, which uses up one block; shift-click takes as many as the stone and the room in your inventory allow. Another stone in the slot clears the choice. Whatever is left in the slot comes back to you when you close the screen. An iron ingot over three stone makes a stonecutter.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-smelting"></a>Furnace and fuel

**Smelting**

A furnace takes something to smelt on top and fuel below; the result appears on the right. It burns one fuel item at a time while it has work, and keeps working while you are away as long as its area is loaded. Breaking it drops what is inside.

**Smoker and blast furnace**

Two furnaces do one job each, twice as fast and burning their fuel twice as fast: the **smoker** (a furnace with four logs around it) cooks only food, the **blast furnace** (a furnace with five iron ingots and three smooth stone) smelts only raw metal. Everything else still needs the ordinary furnace. The table of furnace recipes shows which machines take each recipe.

**Campfire**

A **campfire** (sticks, coal or charcoal and three logs) gives light and cooks without fuel: use raw meat, fish or a potato on it to lay it on the fire, up to four at a time; after 30 seconds each pops off cooked. It hurts whoever stands in it but does not spread fire. Put it out with a shovel or a bucket of water (what lies on it drops) and light it again with flint and steel.

**Metals**

Every metal goes the same way: ore → raw metal → ingot in the furnace. Nine ingots make a storage block and one ingot makes nine nuggets; both craft back. Iron goes into tools, armour, buckets, rails, doors, bars and, with its nuggets, chains and lanterns; gold into golden tools and powered rails; copper into doors and trapdoors that open by hand and by signal, bars, chains and lanterns.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-hunger"></a>Food and hunger

**Eating**

The food bar holds 20 points. Hold food and use it (right mouse button) to eat when you are not full. Walking, sprinting, swimming, jumping, mining, fighting and being hurt make you hungry: every 4 points of exhaustion cost one food point. Walking empties a full bar in about 124 min, sprinting in about 8 min; jumps, mining and fights cost a little extra. Healing one health point costs 0.5 food points.

**Cake**

A **cake** (three milk buckets, two sugar, an egg and three wheat; the buckets come back empty) is placed like a block and eaten where it stands: each use while you are hungry eats one of its seven pieces for 2 food points, also with something else in your hand. Others can share it. Once placed it cannot be picked up again; breaking it gives nothing.

**Healing and starving**

With at least 18 food points you heal one health point every 4 s. With an empty food bar you lose one health point every 4 s, but hunger alone never takes you below 1.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-farming"></a>Farming

**Farmland**

Use a hoe on grass or dirt with air above it to make farmland; every tilled block wears the hoe once (not in creative mode). Water within four blocks around it, at the same height or one above, keeps farmland wet. Without water it dries out; dry farmland without a crop on it turns back into dirt, and so does farmland under a full block. Walking and jumping on farmland do not trample it.

**Crops**

Plant wheat seeds, carrots or potatoes on farmland. Crops grow in stages on random ticks (each block gets one about every 68 s) while their chunk is loaded; time while the world is closed does not count. They grow twice as fast on wet farmland and need light of at least 9, from the sky or from lamps. Break a ripe crop for the harvest; an unripe one only gives back what was planted. Seeds come from tall grass. Growing wheat, carrots and potatoes are found only in the fields of villages: harvest a ripe plant there for your first carrots and potatoes (a zombie now and then drops one too).

**Pumpkins and melons**

Pumpkin and melon seeds grow into stems. A ripe stem puts its fruit onto a free neighbouring block of farmland, dirt or grass, one at a time; harvest the fruit and the stem grows another. Leave a free side next to every stem. A pumpkin gives four seeds, a melon slice one; pumpkins and melons also grow wild.

**Seeds plant themselves**

Seeds, carrots and potatoes lying on free farmland plant one of their stack by themselves (checked every 1 s, with a high chance). A stack whose own block is taken, or that holds more, plants into the free farmland right around it, one seed per check, so drops that merged into one stack still plant the whole patch. Flowing water carries dropped items along its current; still water does not. A field washed off with water from behind replants itself where the drops come to rest; collect the rest of the harvest.

**Bone meal**

A bone from a skeleton makes three bone meal. Used on a crop that is not ripe yet, it grows 2–5 stages at once; on a sapling it has a 45 % chance to let the tree grow right away, if it has room. Each use takes one bone meal, except in creative mode.

**Breeding animals**

Feed pigs with carrots or potatoes and sheep with wheat: a fed adult falls in love for a while and walks towards another one of its kind in love; two that see each other get a young one. Parents rest five minutes before they breed again; a young one grows up in twenty minutes, each feeding takes 10 % of that time off. Young animals leave nothing behind. Crowded places get no more young ones.

**Food from the farm**

Bread from three wheat, baked potatoes from the furnace, pumpkin pie from a pumpkin, sugar and wheat, melon slices and carrots to eat on the way. Carrots and potatoes are planted when used on farmland and eaten elsewhere.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-signal"></a>Signal Dust

**The wire**

Signal Dust comes from Signal ore deep below (an iron pickaxe or better). Laid on a block it becomes a wire: it connects to the dust next to it, climbs up the side of a block when nothing opaque lies on it, and runs down the side of a block that is not opaque. The signal only goes down a step from dust on a block that conducts signals (not glass, slabs or the Signal Dust block): dust on glass or an upper slab passes it up a stair but not down. A lone dust is a dot that powers all four sides; one connection makes a straight line that points on; more make corners and crosses. A signal starts with strength 15 and loses one per block of wire, so after fifteen blocks it is gone; a Signal Repeater starts it at 15 again.

**Powered blocks**

Full solid blocks like stone carry power on. A lever or button powers the block it hangs on strongly, a plate the block below, a Signal Torch the block above, a repeater the block in front. A strongly powered block powers dust and slots around it. Dust powers the block it lies on and the blocks its lines point at only weakly: a weakly powered block still powers lamps, pistons, doors, torches and repeaters next to it, but never other dust. Glass, pistons and slabs carry nothing.

**Sources**

A lever stays on or off. A stone button gives a signal for one second, a wooden one for one and a half. A wooden pressure plate is pressed by anything lying or standing on it, a stone one only by players and creatures; it springs back about a second after it is free. A Signal Torch is on unless its block is powered, two ticks after the change: an inverter. Switched on and off more than eight times within three seconds it burns out for eight seconds. A Small Signal Torch does the same with strength 7 and does not power a block above it, only a component sitting right on it. A Signal Repeater passes a signal from back to front after one to four steps of two ticks (use it to change the delay); a short pulse comes out as long as its delay, and a powered repeater pointing at its side locks it.

**Tripwire**

Hang a tripwire hook on the side of a block and a second one facing it up to 40 blocks away at the same height, then lay string between them on the ground: the string becomes tripwire and the hooks click into place. Whatever touches the wire (a player, a creature, an arrow, an item) sets both hooks off: they give strength 15 to the dust and components next to them and strongly into the block they hang on, until about half a second after the wire is free again. Tearing an armed wire off by hand sets the hooks off once; cut it with shears to take it away quietly. An iron ingot over a stick over planks makes two hooks, an iron nugget one.

**Trapped chest**

A trapped chest holds as much as a chest and gives as much signal as players are looking into it (at most 15), to the dust and components next to it and strongly into the block below. A comparator reads its contents like a chest's. Craft it from a chest and a tripwire hook.

**Slots**

What reacts to a signal is called a slot. A Signal Lamp lights at once and goes dark a moment after it loses power. Doors, trapdoors and fence gates open when their power comes and close when it goes; in between players still open and close them by hand. Iron doors and iron trapdoors open only by signal, not by hand.

**Pistons**

A powered Signal Piston pushes up to 12 blocks in front of it by one. Chests, barrels and furnaces move with everything in them. Plants, torches, dust and liquids in the way break; bedrock, obsidian and extended pistons stop it. Players, creatures, items and armour stands in the way are pushed along. A Sticky Signal Piston pulls the block in front of its head back when it retracts. A piston is not powered through its front. Levers, buttons, torches and dust on its back, sides, top or bottom stay when it extends; what hangs on the first pushed block or on a pulled block drops; on blocks further along it currently stays.

**Hoppers**

A Signal Hopper moves 2.5 items a second: it passes one item from its first slot that fits into the container its spout points at, then takes one from the container above it. Without a container above it collects items lying in its bowl or on the block above it, also through a slab, a carpet, farmland or any block that does not fill its cell; under a full block it collects nothing. Into a furnace it puts items from above into the input and from the side into the fuel slot (fuel only); from below it takes the furnace's output. Any signal locks it. A hopper with nothing to do rests until something around it changes, so idle hoppers cost nothing. Several hoppers that put items into one full container at the same time: the one from above first, then the others by item, equal items all or none.

**Comparators**

A Signal Comparator passes on the strength from its back, two ticks later. A chest, barrel, hopper, dispenser or furnace behind it (also behind one full block) gives its fullness instead: nothing in it is 0, otherwise 1 plus 14 times how full its slots are on average. Ripe wheat, carrots and potatoes are read too (0 to 15 by their stage). In compare mode a stronger side input (dust, a Signal Dust block, a repeater or comparator pointing at it) switches it off; in subtract mode the side input is taken off. Use it to switch the mode. A hopper sorter whose filter is read by a comparator needs an inverter to lock the hopper below the filter: a Signal Torch burns out when one kind of item comes at full hopper speed (eight times off within three seconds) and the filter then drains; a comparator in subtract mode with a lever behind it does not burn out (the showcase's sorter).

**Observers**

A Signal Observer watches the block in front of its face. When that block changes its state (placed, broken, a crop grows, dust changes its strength, a door opens), it sends a pulse of two ticks out of its back, two ticks later. Changes of what is inside a container do not count. Two observers looking at each other make a fast clock.

**Dispensers and droppers**

When their power comes, a Signal Dispenser and a Signal Dropper eject one item from a random slot four ticks later (powered like a piston, also through the block above). A dropper facing a container puts the item into it, otherwise it throws it out. A dispenser pours water and lava buckets, takes a liquid with an empty bucket, puts armour on a player or armour stand in front, shoots arrows and splash potions straight ahead and throws everything else out, also in front of a container. Seeds, carrots and potatoes are thrown, never planted: they plant themselves when they land on free farmland.

**Known quirks**

Pistons are also powered by whatever would power the block above them, but only notice it when a block next to them changes, or a Signal Torch or dust two blocks away (next to one of their neighbours): a piston can sit powered and still until then (a block update detector, BUD). The game rule quasiConnectivity switches this off for a world; a block can refuse it in its data, as the decoupled pistons, dispenser and dropper do: an anvil turns a whole stack of them into the ordinary ones and back for one Signal Dust chunk and one level per started 16 blocks. A pulse shorter than a piston's two-tick extension (down to one that comes and goes within a single tick) pushes the block and lets go of it: a sticky piston leaves its block one further (block spitting). Torch clocks burn out, and repeaters keep their delays.

**Same in every place**

A circuit behaves the same at every coordinate and in every rotation. Everything that switches in the same step decides on the world as it was before the step and switches together. When two pistons want the same block or cell in the same step, both stay where they are.

**On a server**

Circuits run on the server; everyone sees the same states. Levers, buttons and repeaters switch at most 5 times a second per player. The work of all circuits per tick is limited; a huge machine slows down instead of stopping the server.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-rails"></a>Rails and Minecarts

**Laying track**

Rails need a solid block below them. A rail joins the rails beside it on its own: one neighbour makes a straight piece that points at it, two at a right angle make a curve, a line of three or four keeps the straight line (a branch does not bend it). A rail with a rail one block higher beside it rises towards it, so a track climbs and descends one block at a time. Where the neighbours decide nothing, a new rail runs the way you look. Rails behave the same in every direction.

**Minecarts**

Use a minecart on a rail to set it down. A cart keeps rolling along the track, round curves and over slopes: downhill it speeds up, uphill it slows down and may roll back, and it slowly loses speed on the flat (a ridden cart much more slowly than an empty one). At the end of a track it stops in front of a block and flies on otherwise (see below). Carts that run into each other pass their speed on; walking into a cart pushes it. A cart without rails under it stops after a short slide, or coasts far on ice. One hit breaks a cart; it drops itself.

**Riding**

Use a cart to get in and sneak to get out; you land beside it. While you ride, the forward key pushes the cart along the way you look. The server moves the cart and its rider, so everyone sees you ride the same way.

**Track ends and jumps**

A cart that reaches the end of a track with a block in front of it stops there. At an open end it keeps its speed and flies on: it falls, and off the top of a ramp it jumps. Where it lands it stops after a short slide, coasts far on ice, or, landing on a rail, rolls on along it with its speed along the track (landing across the track it stands until the next cart bumps it on). A rider flies with the cart and takes no fall damage.

**Pistons and carts**

A Signal Piston pushes a cart in front of its head or in front of the block it moves, like any other body; a piston pushing a line of rails moves the rails and the cart on them. A rider stays in the cart.

**Powered, detector and activator rails**

Three rails work with Signal Dust. A powered rail speeds a cart up while it gets power, up to the top speed, and brakes it to a stop while it does not; a cart standing on a powered rail with a block at one end starts away from the block. A detector rail gives a signal while a cart is on it; a Signal Comparator behind it reads the contents of a chest or hopper cart on it. An activator rail with power puts the rider out and stops a hopper cart pulling; without power it lets hopper carts pull again. Power given to one powered or activator rail passes along the line of rails of the same kind for eight rails, also up and down slopes, and goes when the power goes. These rails do not bend: where two rails meet at a corner they keep their line. Everything works the same in every direction.

**Chest and hopper carts**

A chest cart carries a chest: use it to open its screen like a chest. A hopper cart carries a Signal Hopper and pulls one item at a time from a container above its rail, or picks up items lying on its rail; it rests between items like a hopper. A Signal Hopper under a rail takes the items out of a chest or hopper cart standing above it, one at a time, and passes them on; Signal Hoppers can carry rails. Broken, a cart drops itself and what it carried. Carts keep their contents when their chunk is saved or unloaded.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-health"></a>Health and damage

**Health**

You have 20 health points, shown as hearts. Falls deeper than 3 m cost one point per further metre; landing in water or on a ladder is safe. Hostile creatures, lava and starving hurt as well. After a hit you cannot be hurt for 0.5 s, unless a later hit is stronger than the strongest so far: it deals only the difference. Creatures are protected the same way.

**Fighting**

Left-click a creature within 4 m to hit it, at most every 0.4 s. Swords and axes deal more damage than the hand; every hit knocks the target back.

**Status effects**

Some attacks leave an effect that lasts a while. Next to the hearts a box shows each effect with its picture, name and the time left. Poison, from the bite of a cave spider, takes one health point every 1.25 s (faster at higher levels) but never your last one, and turns the hearts green. Armour does not help against it. Effects end with time or when you die; zombies, skeletons and spiders are immune to poison.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-fire"></a>Fire

**Burning**

Fire stands on a solid block or next to a flammable one, and it lights everything around it with light 15. About every 1.5 to 1.95 seconds it takes a step and grows older; after 45 steps at the latest it goes out. Without a flammable block next to it, it goes out after 9 steps, so fire on stone or sand burns down quickly. Fire whose ground or fuel goes away goes out at once, and water flowing in washes it away. Breaking fire puts it out.

**Lighting fire**

Flint and steel lights fire in front of the face of a block you use it on, if fire can stand there: on a solid block or next to a flammable one. Each fire wears it by one use; in creative mode it does not wear. A Signal Dispenser holding flint and steel lights the cell in front of it when it fires; if nothing can burn there, the flint and steel stays inside. Lava now and then sets fire to the air right next to it when that air touches a flammable block, but only when something next to the lava changes or it flows: a still lava lake does not set the woods around it alight. Every new fire is a spark of its own and keeps the limits below. Breaking or punching fire puts it out, also in creative mode.

**Spreading**

Each step may burn away the flammable blocks next to the fire: they turn into fire or into air, and nothing drops. It may also light the air around it next to flammable blocks, most readily above it. How readily a block catches fire and how quickly it burns away depends on the block: leaves, wool, grass and flowers burn readily, logs, planks and coal blocks slowly; stone, earth, glass and metal never burn. Fire on a magma block burns forever without spreading: a hearth. A new fire is as old as the one that lit it, so a fire front always dies out.

**Limits**

Fire spreads at most 12 blocks from the spark it began with, so a single spark never burns down a whole forest or village. It never spreads into chunks that are not loaded. A chunk holds at most 256 fires and the world 4096; beyond, no new fire is lit. The game rule `fireSpread` (`/gamerule fireSpread false`) stops fire from spreading and burning blocks away; fires then only burn where they are until they go out. The showcase world has it off. A server can also switch spreading off for all its worlds with the limit `fireSpreadRadius` 0 in server.json.

**Burning players and creatures**

Whoever walks into fire or lava catches fire and keeps burning for a while after stepping out (fire 8 seconds, lava 15; see the block pages). While burning, a player or creature loses 1 health every 1 second; armour takes part of it like any blow. Water puts the flames out at once, and so does rain falling on you. Players in creative mode do not burn. Items that fall into fire or lava burn up.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-weather"></a>Weather

**Clear, rain and thunderstorms**

The weather is the same in the whole world. A clear spell lasts 5 to 25 minutes; then it rains for 2.5 to 10 minutes, or, with a chance of 25 in 100, a thunderstorm rages for 2 to 6 minutes; after that it clears up again. Rain and thunder come and go over 10 seconds: clouds cover the sky, it grows darker, the fog comes closer and the sky lights the land less. Each world has its own course of weather, given by its seed.

**Rain and snow**

Rain and snow fall only where the sky is open: not under a roof, a tree or an overhang. In cold biomes (taiga, tundra) it snows instead of raining, and in every other biome it snows from 150 blocks up (in the mountains from their snow line). In the desert it stays dry, although the sky darkens there too. You hear the rain around you, muffled under a roof.

**Thunderstorms**

In a thunderstorm lightning strikes now and then within 48 blocks of a player, on average every 10 seconds per player, only where it rains (not into snow or the desert). The flash lights up the sky at once; the thunder follows as late as sound needs for the distance: about three seconds per kilometre. A strike hurts everyone within 3 blocks by 5 health (armour takes part of it) and sets them alight; it may light a few fires where it strikes, but only while fire spreads (`fireSpread`) and within the limits of fire.

**What rain and snow do**

Rain puts out the fires it falls on, sooner or later (the older the fire, the sooner), and fire does not spread into the rain; fire on a magma block burns on. It also puts out burning players and creatures, and it keeps farmland under the open sky wet like water nearby. It never fills the world with water. Where it snows, a thin snow cover settles bit by bit on the tops of solid blocks under the open sky, not under a roof, on ice, glass or leaves, and only one layer per spot. Snow does not melt again.

**Changing the weather**

`/weather` shows the weather. Operators set it with `/weather clear`, `/weather rain` or `/weather thunder`, for a number of seconds (`/weather rain 120`) or as long as the course of the world says. With the game rule **weatherCycle** off (`/gamerule weatherCycle false`) the weather stays as it is until it is set again; the showcase world starts that way, with a clear sky.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-villages"></a>Villages

**Finding villages**

Villages stand on flat land in plains, deserts, taiga and tundra, and on stilts in the wet parts of swamps; never in oceans, mountains or forests. The world is divided into square regions (384 blocks in the base game), and a region holds at most one village, about half a region away from the next. Where a village goes depends only on the seed, so a world of the same seed has its villages in the same places. Operators find the nearest one with `/locate village`; `/village` shows its state.

**Styles**

Each biome builds in its own style. Plains villages are made of oak and cobblestone with pitched roofs of stairs, a well with a bell in the middle, main roads of cobblestone or gravel, side roads of dirt path and lamp posts. Desert villages are made of sandstone and terracotta with flat roofs, main roads of terracotta or packed mud that stand out against the sand, side roads of cut sandstone and fields with sugar cane by the water. Taiga villages are the plains houses in spruce. Snowy villages in the tundra mix igloos of snow blocks with ice windows, spruce cabins under a layer of snow and the larger plains houses in spruce, with main roads of packed ice or gravel. Swamp villages stand in the water on stilts of dark spruce: huts with thatched roofs, a meeting hall with the bell on a large platform in the middle, a fisher's hut, a herb hut with mushrooms and raised fields, all joined by boardwalks at one height above the water, with stairs down where a boardwalk reaches dry land. Around them the swamp is marshland: irregular pools of shallow water ringed by mud, grass between them and sugar cane at the water. A biome that has villages but no style of its own (one a mod adds) builds in the plains style.

**Buildings**

Every village has its own street plan: one long main street through the middle with side streets, four roads from the well to all sides, three in a T or two in an L, a ring road around a square with the well, or roads at all angles that bend; some roads wind along the slopes. Diagonal roads run as staircases, and the houses at them have a forecourt to the road. Roads stop at water and steep slopes. Along them stand small and large houses with beds, fields of wheat, carrots and potatoes, ripe and still growing, with water and paths between the rows, a smithy with furnaces and a church or temple, each with its door to the road. Every kind of house comes in several designs, and no two houses need to look alike: walls of oak, birch or spruce, roofs of wood, cobblestone or brick, a chimney, flowers in front, a shed beside, terracotta in different colours in the desert. Some villages have an animal pen with a pair of pigs or sheep; a village near water has a fisher's hut with a jetty out over the water; large villages have a stone watchtower, taller than every house, with a ladder up to its battlements. Where a main road climbs a terrace by one block, a slab makes the step. Lanterns light the houses inside and lamp posts the roads, so no monsters spawn in them. Houses rest on a foundation where the ground is uneven, and the ground above them is cleared.

**Beds and workstations**

The world remembers every village: its middle, its area, its beds and its workstations (composter: farmer, furnace: smith, barrel: fisher). Beds and workstations you place or break inside a village count at once; one placed up to 32 blocks from a village's buildings joins it, and the area grows, but never into another village's: every bed and workstation belongs to one village. A bed or workstation farther from every village starts a settlement of its own: villagers without a home move in, and it lives like any village (children, golems). A settlement without beds, workstations and villagers is forgotten. Villagers claim a bed and a workstation each (see Villagers). A village lists at most 64 beds and 64 workstations (one placed beyond that is not listed later either); the server limits `villageBeds`, `villageWorkstations` and `villages` change that.

**Villages in mods**

A style is a file in `villages/`: the biomes it builds in, the well, the lamp post, the kinds of buildings with weights and maximum counts, each with one or several designs, the street plans (main street, cross, T, L, ring road, branching roads, winding or straight), the road surfaces for main roads and side roads (one chosen per village, with an optional slab for steps), the crops of the fields and the foundation block and a palette that swaps blocks of the houses. A kind can have rules: a chance per village, a smallest village size (the length of all its roads) or water within reach (then it gets a jetty). A style can stand over water on stilts, with boardwalks for roads and the swamp around it shaped into marshland with pools, mud and grass. The houses are ordinary structures; `choices` (block swaps, one option per house) and optional `parts` with a chance (chimney, flowers, shed) make every house different. A file with `extends` changes a style or makes a new one from it, and its `add` appends biomes, crops, kinds of buildings and street plans, so several mods can each add a crop to the plains. A mod can add a style for its own biome, a new style from the base game's houses with another palette, or new house designs.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-villagers"></a>Villagers

**Homes**

When the part of a village with a bed is seen for the first time, a villager moves into every free bed, up to 32 in one village. Each one claims its bed and keeps it; break the bed and the villager looks for another free one. A villager without a bed, or carried more than 32 blocks beyond its village's buildings (in a minecart, pushed or led away), moves into the village with the free bed nearest to it within 48 blocks. Villagers stay in the world for good: they wait while their part of the world is not loaded and are saved with it. Their clothes follow the style of their village: tunics on the plains, robes and headwraps in the desert, fur in the taiga, pale furs and fur hats in the snow, smocks and waders with reed hats in the swamp, each villager with its own colours.

**Professions**

A villager without work claims a free workstation and takes its profession: the composter makes a farmer (apron and straw hat), the furnace a smith (leather apron) and the barrel a fisher (oilskin vest and hat). Each workstation belongs to one villager. Farmers harvest the ripe crops of every field within 8 blocks of their composter, yours too, and plant them again at once; the harvest stays with the village (no items drop). With the game rule `mobGriefing` off they leave the crops alone. A villager that loses its workstation before it ever traded is out of work again. Villagers with a profession trade with you (see the chapter on trading).

**A villager's day**

After sunrise villagers stroll about, then go to work. Around noon they meet at the bell and go back to work in the afternoon. Towards evening they stroll once more, at sunset they go home, and at night they lie in their beds until morning. A villager in bed gets up when it is hurt. When it rains in the village, everyone goes home. Monsters within 10 blocks send villagers running home; one that is hit runs away too. They can be hurt like animals and do not fight back.

**Children**

A village grows into its free beds. Two grown-up villagers that meet by day, in dry weather, and each have food (at least 3 crops) get a child when their village has a free bed: the child moves into the free bed nearest to them. Farmers bring in the food: what they harvest stays with them, and a farmer with more than 6 crops shares with the villagers near it that have too little. The parents then rest for 5 minutes. A child is half as tall, has no profession, plays about the village instead of working and does not trade; after 20 minutes it is grown up and takes a free workstation. No child is born at night, in rain, without a free bed or beyond 32 villagers in one village and 1024 in the world. Throw them bread (worth 3 crops), carrots, potatoes or wheat and grown-up villagers pick it up as food until they hold 9 crops (not with the game rule `mobGriefing` off). So placing beds in a village makes it grow, and a bed left by a villager who died is filled again.

**Iron golems**

Villagers call an iron golem when they are in danger or gossip, but only those that slept in their bed within the last 10 minutes. A villager panics when a monster comes within 10 blocks, even in its bed, which wakes it. When a panicking villager has at least 3 villagers that slept lately within 10 blocks of it (itself included) and no iron golem is within 16 blocks, a golem appears somewhere within 8 blocks of it, on solid ground where it fits; glass, panes, leaves and ice carry no golem. Without panic, 5 such villagers together gossip a golem up the same way, with a chance of 10 % every 5 seconds. A village summons at most one golem every 30 seconds, and villagers summon none beyond 256 golems in the world. This works with a handful of villagers, so a small pen with three beds and a monster kept behind glass is a golem farm. Villagers summon golems even when creatures do not spawn by themselves. A village golem strolls within its village and walks back when it strays.

**Building and keeping golems**

Build an iron golem yourself: stand four iron blocks upright in a T (two on top of each other, one on each side of the upper one) and put a carved pumpkin on top, facing any way; the blocks beside the head and the foot must be empty. The blocks turn into the golem. Carve a pumpkin by using shears on it; four pumpkin seeds fall out. Iron golems guard villagers: they hunt monsters they see and throw them into the air with their blows. They leave players alone, but a player who hurts a villager or a golem of a village is its enemy for 5 minutes, and a golem that is hurt is angry with the player for 30 seconds; golems you built never attack players. Repair a hurt golem by using an iron ingot on it. A golem drops iron ingots and sometimes poppies.

**Doors and long ways**

Villagers open wooden doors on their way and close them behind them; iron doors stay shut for them. A long way through the village, say across the boardwalks of a swamp village, goes along the roads in stages, so villagers find their way home from every corner of their village.

**Villagers in mods**

A creature kind lives in villages with `"villager": "<settings>"`, naming its species' file in `villagers/`: the daily routine as steps through the day (wander, work, meet, home, sleep), how far it flees, trading, `breeding` (the food a parent needs, how farmers share, how long children take to grow up and parents rest) and the default look. Each profession is its own file in `professions/`: the blocks it works at, layers laid over the outfit, a hat, whether it tends crops, and optionally its own routine; two mods each adding a profession keep both. A village style dresses its villagers with `look` in its file (an outfit, hair styles and hats to choose from and lists of skin, hair, shirt and trouser colours), may name its own villager kind and golems and lists other residents. `worldgen/villages.json` names the villagers and the golem of all other villages and its thresholds.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-trading"></a>Trading

**The trading screen**

Use a villager with a profession to open its trading screen. On the left it lists its offers: what you pay, what you get and how often it can still sell before it restocks. Pick an offer and its costs and result show in large slots; a click on the result trades once, a shift-click as often as your inventory pays (at most 64 times). The price comes out of your inventory; what does not fit into it falls at your feet. While the screen is open the villager stands and faces you, and only you can trade with it. The screen closes when you walk away, when a monster comes near or someone hits the villager (it runs home), and at night villagers in bed do not trade. Villagers without a profession only look at you.

**Levels and experience**

Every trade gives the villager experience; the green bar under its name shows the way to its next level. A novice becomes an apprentice at 10 experience, a journeyman at 70, an expert at 150 and a master at 250. With each level it adds 2 offers of that level, chosen once and kept for good, so two villagers of one profession can offer different things. You get experience too: every trade drops a few experience orbs, more for the offers of higher levels.

**Restocking and prices**

An offer can be traded a limited number of times. During work time a villager at its workstation restocks all its offers, up to 2 times a day. An offer that sold out before a restock is in demand: the first thing it asks for costs a little more the next time, at most twice the usual price. Offers that are not sold out get cheaper again by one step at every restock.

**The farmer**

Buys wheat, potatoes and carrots (novice), pumpkins (apprentice) and melons (journeyman) for an emerald. Sells bread, apples, pumpkin pie, baked potatoes, sugar, hay bales and melon seeds, and as a master mushroom stew and a diamond hoe.

**The smith**

Buys coal and iron ingots (novice), gold ingots (apprentice), lapis lazuli (journeyman) and diamonds (expert). Sells iron nuggets, iron pickaxes and axes, an iron helmet and chestplate, a diamond sword, and as a master a diamond pickaxe and single diamonds.

**The fisher**

Buys string and coal (novice), sugar cane (apprentice), slimeballs (journeyman) and leather (expert). Fills your bucket with water for an emerald, sells lanterns, cooked mutton and fireflies in a bottle, and as a master slime blocks and diamond boots.

**Emeralds**

Emeralds are the villagers' money. You earn them by selling goods, or mine them: emerald ore lies rarely and in very small veins deep in the stone and needs an iron pickaxe or better. Nine emeralds make a block of emerald and back.

**Offers in mods**

A file in `trades/` lists offers of one profession: for each the level from which it is offered, one or two costs and a result (item and count), how often it can be traded until the next restock (`maxUses`), the experience for the villager and for the player and the price rise for demand. A file of another name adds offers to the profession; one of the same name as an earlier pack's replaces it. The experience per level, the number of offers per level and the restocks per day are set under `trading` in the villagers' species file in `villagers/`; a profession may set its own number of offers.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-archery"></a>Archery

**Drawing and shooting**

Hold the use button with a bow in your hand to draw it and let go to shoot. A full draw takes 1 s and sends the arrow off at 60 m/s; a short draw shoots weaker, and a very short one not at all. The bow uses the first arrow of your inventory and wears with every shot.

**Arrows in flight**

Arrows fall in an arc and slow down, much faster in water. A hit deals more damage the faster the arrow flies and pushes the target back; armour protects as against blows. Arrows stick in the block they hit and fall when it goes; walk over them to pick them up. They vanish after 60 s. A world holds at most 1024 arrows in flight or stuck, and at most 64 stuck in one chunk (the oldest go first).

**Throwing**

Some items are thrown by hand: press the use button with a snowball in your hand and it flies off at once. You can throw again after a short moment. A bow shoots only arrows, never snowballs. Snowballs fly in an arc like arrows, deal no damage, push the target back and burst where they hit. A shade pearl is thrown the same way and teleports you to where it lands.

**Fishing**

Cast a fishing rod with the use button and its bobber flies off and floats on the water. After 5 to 30 s a fish bites (a quarter sooner in the rain): the bobber dips and splashes, and for 1 s you can reel in with the use button to catch it. Mostly you catch cod and salmon, now and then a stick, string, leather, a bone, a bowl or even a bow. Reel in too early or too late and you catch nothing. The bobber is gone when you select another slot or walk more than 32 m away, and it is never saved with the world.

**See also**

[Bow](items.md#item-qtcraft-bow), [Arrow](items.md#item-qtcraft-arrow), [Feather](items.md#item-qtcraft-feather), [Snowball](items.md#item-qtcraft-snowball), [Shade Pearl](items.md#item-qtcraft-shade_pearl), [Fishing rod](items.md#item-qtcraft-fishing_rod)

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-explosives"></a>Explosives

**Explosives and gunpowder**

Gunpowder comes from coal and flint (two per craft); five gunpowder and four sand make an explosive block. Coastal towers keep some in their chests. An explosive breaks at once and drops itself, so it can be carried away safely.

**Lighting**

Flint and steel used on an explosive lights it, so does a dispenser with flint and steel facing it, any signal power (a lever, a button, a torch, a pressure plate on top of it, a powered block next to it), fire that reaches it and the blast of another explosion. A lit explosive hops up a little, falls like sand, hisses and flashes, and explodes after 4 seconds; one lit by another explosion goes off sooner, after half a second to a second and a half.

**The blast**

The blast reaches out in every direction and each block it meets soaks up some of its force by its blast resistance (see the block pages): dirt, sand, glass and wood break easily, stone and bricks hold more, and obsidian never breaks. Liquids soak up the whole blast: an explosive under water breaks no block. A block that breaks drops its item only now and then (one in four for an explosive); chests drop what they hold. The same explosion in the same place of a world always leaves the same crater.

**Players and creatures**

Players and creatures up to 8 blocks away are hurt and thrown back, the more the closer they are and the more of them the blast can see: right next to it up to 57 damage (armour helps), behind a solid wall nothing at all. In creative mode nothing hurts.

**Limits and the game rule**

However big a field of explosives, the world stays playable: at most 512 explosives burn at once (beyond, a lit one stays a block until there is room), at most 8 explode per tick (the others wait a tick), one explosion breaks at most 1024 blocks, the nearest first, and all explosions together change at most 2048 blocks per tick, the rest in the next ticks. The game rule `explosionBlockDamage` (on by default; `/gamerule explosionBlockDamage false`) keeps every block: explosions still hurt and push, but break and light nothing. Server operators tune the limits in the limits of server.json (`primedExplosives`, `explosionsPerTick`, `explosionBlocks`, `explosionBlocksPerTick`).

**See also**

[Explosive](blocks.md#block-qtcraft-explosive), [Gunpowder](items.md#item-qtcraft-gunpowder), [Flint and steel](items.md#item-qtcraft-flint_and_steel), [Obsidian](blocks.md#block-qtcraft-obsidian), [Stone pressure plate](blocks.md#block-qtcraft-stone_pressure_plate), [Lever](blocks.md#block-qtcraft-lever)

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-death"></a>Death and respawn

**When you die**

At zero health you die. Respawn takes you back to the spawn point with full health and food. With the game rule keepInventory (on by default) you keep your items; otherwise they scatter where you died.

**The void**

Nothing holds you below the bottom of the world: through a hole in the bedrock or off the edge of an empty world you fall into the void. From 64 blocks below the bottom the void hurts you by 4 points every 0.5 s, whatever armour you wear, and in creative mode too, even while flying. Dying there is “fell out of the world”; items, creatures and anything else that falls that deep are gone for good.

**Dropped items**

Items on the ground vanish after 300 s while their area is loaded. Items you throw can be picked up again after 2 s.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-experience"></a>Experience

**Experience orbs**

Mining ores (coal, diamond, lapis lazuli, Signal Dust ore), killing creatures, smelting in a furnace and breeding animals give experience as small glowing orbs. Orbs fly to the nearest player within 8 blocks and are collected with a chime; a furnace keeps the experience of what it smelted until a player takes the result. Orbs close together merge into one, and they vanish after 300 s while their area is loaded. A world holds at most 1024 orbs; beyond that new experience joins the nearest orb, so nothing is lost.

**Levels**

Experience fills the green bar above the hotbar; when it is full you reach the next level, shown by the number in the middle, with a fanfare. Each level costs more than the one before: 7 points for the first, 17 for level 5, 37 for level 15 and 112 for level 30. Levels are what traders and enchanting will ask for.

**Experience and death**

When you die you drop 7 points per level as orbs, at most 100 points; the rest is lost and you start again at level 0. With the game rule keepInventory you keep your experience, and so do players in creative mode.

**The /xp command**

Operators change experience with /xp add <player> <amount> [points|levels] (a negative amount takes away), /xp set <player> <amount> [points|levels] and /xp query <player>.

**See also**

[Lapis lazuli ore](blocks.md#block-qtcraft-lapis_ore), [Furnace](blocks.md#block-qtcraft-furnace)

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-creatures"></a>Creatures

**Where creatures appear**

Monsters appear 24–128 m away from players wherever it is dark enough: no torch or lamp may light the spot at all, and the sky above must be dark. At night most open ground passes, the more often the darker it is; by day only caves and the deep shade under roofs and very dense canopies are dark enough, and a thunderstorm darkens the day as much. Like every walking creature they stand on solid, opaque blocks with a full top: never on glass, leaves, ice, a lower slab, a path or farmland, and never in rails. Peaceful animals appear 20–48 m away on grass by day. Creatures that spawn in caves need at least 8 blocks of rock above. Each page lists the blocks, biomes, time and light a creature needs.

**Monsters of the biomes**

Every biome has its list of monsters, each with a weight: zombies, skeletons, spiders, slimes and Kjuts 100 each, shadewalkers 10. Witches are in no list: they spawn only in the footprint of their huts in the swamp, which takes the place of the list there. Each try to spawn a monster picks one of the whole list by weight and then checks whether that kind may appear at the spot; if not, nothing comes. So slimes take their share only in their chunks and caves, and a kind that needs water or the open sky leaves the others' share as it is. In the desert husks take 80 of the zombies' 100 and appear only under the open sky; they do not burn in the sun, and their blows make you hungry. In the snowy plains (tundra) strays take 80 of the skeletons' 100, also only under the open sky; their arrows slow you down. Drowned rise from dark water: in rivers with weight 100, in frozen rivers 1, elsewhere 5 and only in water at least six blocks below the sea level. Cave spiders come only from the spawners of mine shafts. Each biome's page shows the shares of its monsters.

**Burning in daylight**

Zombies, skeletons, strays and drowned catch fire in direct sunlight and burn for 8 s after they leave it. Shade, a roof or leaves above their heads, water and rain protect them, and so do caves. Husks never burn.

**Spiders and cave spiders**

Spiders climb straight up walls and hang on to overhangs, so a wall alone does not keep them out; an overhang of one block at the top stops the wide spider, not the small cave spider. By day in the open a spider leaves you alone until you hit it; at night and in caves it hunts you and leaps at you. Cave spiders live deep in dark caves, always attack and poison you with their bite. They fit through gaps one block high.

**Silverfish and infested stone**

In the mountains some stone is infested: it looks like stone but breaks much faster, even by hand, and drops nothing; a silverfish crawls out. Hit a silverfish and it calls its kin out of the infested blocks up to ten blocks around. Left alone, a silverfish slips into a stone, cobblestone or stone brick block next to it and waits there. Breaking blocks in creative mode lets nothing out.

**Kjuts and their children**

Kjuts float out of dark places like other monsters. Each hit a big Kjut survives splits off a little one, up to four at once and eight in all; a snowball or an egg splits nothing. The little ones circle it on glowing threads until they see you. The little ones belong to it like the children of a Qt object to their parent: when the parent dies, they all pop at once and leave gunpowder; killed one by one, they leave only spark gel. So for gunpowder, hit the big one a few times and then finish it; for gel, fight the little ones first. A Kjut that leaves the world without dying takes its little ones along quietly.

**Chickens and eggs**

Chickens flutter down slowly instead of falling and lay an egg every five to ten minutes. Thrown eggs burst where they land; one in eight hatches a chick, and one hatching in 32 brings four. Chickens fall in love with seeds of any kind. They drop feathers for arrows and raw chicken, which you cook in a furnace.

**Shadewalkers**

Shadewalkers come out on dark nights, rarely and in every biome. Look at their heads and they get angry, and the farther away they are, the more exactly you have to look; look at their legs or past them and they stay calm. One you hit chases you, not whoever stands nearest. They teleport to a safe place nearby: when they stand in water or the rain, when a projectile hits them and sometimes to reach the player they chase. They only ever land on solid ground with room above and only where the world is loaded. With the game rule **mobGriefing** they pick up single blocks and put them down elsewhere; a shadewalker carrying a block stays in the world until it puts it down. Without the rule it changes no blocks and drops a block it carries as an item.

**Cows, milk and fish**

Use an empty bucket on a grown cow to milk it. Drinking the milk with the use button ends every status effect, poison too, and gives the bucket back. Cows fall in love with wheat and drop leather and beef. Cod live in lakes and seas, salmon in the cold waters of taiga, tundra and mountains. Fish swim in schools and only in water; on land they flop about and suffocate after 15 seconds. A water bucket used on a fish catches it alive; pouring the fish bucket out lets it go again, and a released fish stays with the world. Fish have their own spawn limit and take no room from the animals on land.

**Wolves, dogs and cats**

Wolves roam forests, taiga and tundra in packs: strike one, and every wolf of the pack nearby chases you. Feed a wolf bones to tame it; each bone works with a chance of one in three, and the tamed wolf becomes your dog with a red collar. Cats live in villages and are tamed the same way with raw cod. A pet belongs to you, not to your character on one login: it stays yours when you leave and waits, standing or sitting, until you come back. Use your pet with an empty hand or any other item to make it sit or stand up. A pet that does not sit follows you when you walk away and, when you are farther than 12 m, teleports next to you, but only onto safe ground in loaded chunks. A dog fights what hurts you and what you hit, never you and never your other pets. Meat heals a dog, fish a cat; a dye colours a dog's collar. Ocelots are shy and run from anyone who comes close, unless you hold raw cod and sneak up slowly.

**Limits**

Monsters share one cap: the world holds at most 70 of them for every 289 loaded chunks within 8 chunks of a player, so players far apart each bring their own share and players close together share one, and a monster only appears where a player nearby has fewer than 70 around. Near each player there are at most 6 peaceful and 10 ambient creatures. Monsters and ambient creatures vanish as soon as every player is farther than 128 m away; once no player has come within 32 m of one for 30 s, it vanishes by chance, after 40 s more on average. Peaceful animals stay: about one in ten new chunks brings a group of them, they wait while their area is not loaded and are saved with the world. New ones only appear near you while no animal is around. Hostile creatures only go after players they see, and follow one they lost sight of for a few seconds. Many creatures in one block cell think less to save work: from 8 on only now and then and without looking for a way, from 24 on not at all; they still fall, are pushed apart and strike a player right next to them.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-world"></a>The world

**Day and night**

A full day lasts 600 s. The world is generated from its seed while you explore it; only what you change is saved.

**Water and lava**

Water and lava flow from their sources; a bucket takes a source and pours it elsewhere. Where lava meets water it hardens into obsidian or cobblestone. Lava lights its surroundings, burns items and hurts.

**Slippery ground**

Ice, packed ice and blue ice are slippery. On them you speed up slowly and keep sliding when you let go, about two blocks from a walk on ice and a little farther on blue ice; your top speed stays the same. Creatures and dropped items slide too, and creatures walk round ice where the way round is short. A minecart off the rails coasts far on ice, while on other ground it stops after a short slide.

**Mine shafts**

Abandoned mine shafts lie deep underground: long corridors of wooden supports with many branches and crossings, reaching up to some 70 blocks from a central room, on two to four levels joined by staircases. Some corridors carry rails, and now and then a chest minecart with loot stands on them. Where a corridor crosses a cave, it runs on a plank bridge held up by pillars. Cobwebs hang in the corners, thickest around the spawners of cave spiders. Dead ends sometimes lead to a storeroom. Some mine shafts come up to the surface: look for a small wooden headframe with a ladder going down, or a timbered tunnel sloping into a hillside, sometimes with rails, most often in the mountains.

**Desert temples and sand golems**

Desert temples stand alone in the desert, and some desert villages are built around one. Steps lead up to the entrance between two towers; ladders in the towers climb to the galleries above the colonnades at the sides and on to the roof. The hall under the dome has a pattern of orange and blue terracotta in its floor. Below the pattern lies a hidden chamber: four chests hold gold, emeralds and now and then diamonds, but the pressure plate in the middle of its floor sets off the explosives beneath it, so dig down beside it rather than falling onto it; a monster that appears in the dark chamber can set it off as well. A ladder at the back of the hall leads down into the dark cellar, guarded by a sand golem. It attacks whoever comes within a few blocks of its cellar and returns there. A sand golem that touches a water source block crumbles into four blocks of sand and a carved sandstone head; a block without room drops as an item (with the game rule mobGriefing off the whole build drops as items). Its arms fall as sand and can fill the water they land in. While fewer golems guard the cellar than it holds (one), a new one appears in the cellar after 120 s (in a cellar seen for the first time since the world was loaded, after 60 s), as long as the cellar is dark (block light at most 7), no player is closer than 12 blocks and one is within 48 blocks. A sand farm: dig holes one block deep into the cellar floor, pour water into them, keep the cellar dark and wait a little away from it; a new golem appears on the dry floor beside the water and crumbles at once (a cellar full of water stays empty). Its arms stand beside its middle, left and right of where it looks, and fall to the floor: a hole right beside its feet fills with sand. Place the holes diagonally next to the golem's spot (it touches them, its arms miss them) or keep the falling sand off them with a block. That gives at most 120 blocks of sand and 30 heads per hour and temple. The sand fills the cellar until you dig it out.

**Jungle temples**

Jungle temples stand alone in the jungle, overgrown with moss and vines. Climb down the ladder at the back of the hall: a corridor with a stone pressure plate leads to a room with three levers on its wall. Stepping on the plate makes the dispenser beside it shoot an arrow at you. Two of the levers sit on dispensers that shoot arrows into the room; the left one opens the hidden chamber with two chests next to the room (a signal torch behind the wall holds two sticky pistons out, and the lever puts it out). Dispensers shoot arrows and other ammunition as projectiles you can pick up again.

**Witch huts and witches**

Witch huts stand on stilts in the swamp, often over water, with a cauldron and potted plants inside. Witches spawn there and only there: where monsters may appear in a column of a hut's footprint, at any height from the bottom of the world to the top, only a witch comes, as often as any monster would and under the same rules: dark enough, 24–128 m from players, within the monster limit, on solid ground with room for her. Floors built into the footprint above or below the hut give more of them; light keeps them away. A witch throws splash potions: poison while you are not poisoned, then harming. A splash reaches everything living around the place it bursts, the weaker the further away. Hurt, she drinks a potion of healing for a moment and throws nothing meanwhile; milk takes the poison away again.

**Ruined portals, wells and oases**

Here and there the remains of an old portal stand in the open: a broken frame of obsidian and crying obsidian amid red rubble and magma, with fallen blocks around it and a chest of gold tools, gold armour, flint and obsidian beside it. Small ruins stand in many biomes, sunken ones are half buried in the ground, large ones stand in mountains, forests, taiga and tundra; no two are quite alike. The portal leads nowhere. Crying obsidian glows faintly (light 10) and, like obsidian, needs a diamond pickaxe. In plains and birch forests, and in sandstone in the desert, wells stand alone: a shaft of water sources four blocks deep under a little roof, a source of water far from any lake. Rare oases lie in the desert: a pool with sand at its bottom, grass, sugar cane and two palm-like trees.

**Coastal towers**

Old stone towers stand on the coasts, on low land with the open sea close by. A ladder in one corner leads up through every floor to the top room, where a chest holds gunpowder, explosives, iron, gold, food and now and then an emerald or a diamond. Beware: the stone pressure plate in front of the chest sits on explosives hidden in the floor, and stepping on it sets them off. Open the chest from the side, or break the plate first.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-maps"></a>Maps

**Making a map**

Lay a **compass** in the middle of the crafting table with eight **paper** around it to get an **empty map**. Using it (**R**) turns it into a map of the land around you. Every map covers a fixed square of the world: at the start 128 × 128 blocks, one block per pixel, lined up on a grid, so two maps made near each other show the same square. Each map has its own number, shown when you point at it in the inventory.

**Exploring**

A map draws only while you hold it: the land up to 128 blocks around you appears within a second, seen from above with the colour of each top block. Slopes facing north are darker and those facing south brighter; deep water is darker than shallow water. What nobody has seen yet stays blank paper. You are the arrow on the map, pointing where you look; other players who carry the same map are arrows too, and a player beyond the edge is a dot at the edge.

**Zooming out**

A filled map in the middle of the crafting table with eight **paper** around it becomes a new, empty map one step larger: twice as many blocks across, up to four steps (2048 × 2048 blocks, sixteen blocks per pixel). The larger map covers the old square and starts blank; explore it again.

**Copies**

A filled map with empty maps in the crafting grid gives one copy per empty map. Copies share the map number and the picture: whatever one holder explores appears on every copy, so a group can share one map.

**Maps on the wall**

Put a filled map into an **item frame** and it covers the whole face of the frame, drawn flat on the wall. Each right click turns it a quarter turn. Maps in frames side by side meet without a gap, so four maps of neighbouring squares make a large wall map. Players near a framed map see it change as others explore, and every map shows the frames it hangs in as green arrows.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-backpack"></a>Backpack

**Making and placing**

Lay a **chest** in the middle of the crafting table with eight **leather** around it to make a **backpack**. Placed, it is a small container with one row of nine slots: use it like a chest. Broken, it keeps everything inside and drops as one backpack that carries its contents; place it again and they are back in its slots. Pointing at a backpack in the inventory lists what it holds. Backpacks never stack, an empty one neither.

**Opening it from the hand**

Hold a backpack and use it (**R**) while you point at nothing: it opens in your hand. You see its nine slots and your hotbar, but not the rest of your inventory; the backpack's own slot in the hotbar is locked while it is open. Shift-click moves a stack between the backpack and the hotbar.

**What goes in**

Anything but another backpack: no backpack fits into a backpack, neither by hand nor by shift-click. Signal Hoppers fill a placed backpack from above and empty it from below like a chest, but never put a backpack into it. A backpack with something inside is no fuel and no ingredient, and villagers do not take it as payment, so nothing inside is lost by accident.

**See also**

[Backpack](blocks.md#block-qtcraft-backpack), [Leather](items.md#item-qtcraft-leather), [Chest](blocks.md#block-qtcraft-chest)

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-creative"></a>Creative mode

**Survival and creative**

Every player has a game mode. In **survival** you gather, craft, eat and can die. In **creative** you build freely: every block and item without limit, flying, blocks break at the first strike, nothing hurts you and you never get hungry. A new world asks for the mode of its players in the main menu (**Game mode**); operators change it with `/gamemode creative` or `/gamemode survival` (also for another player: `/gamemode creative Alice`), and `/defaultgamemode` sets the mode of players who join for the first time.

**Flying**

Tap **Space** twice quickly to take off or to stop flying. While flying, **Space** rises, **Shift** sinks and **Ctrl** doubles the speed (10.9 m/s, sprinting 21.6 m/s); the view widens a little. Sinking onto the ground lands you and ends the flight. The showcase world has a platform high above the ground that only flyers reach.

**Creative inventory**

**E** opens the creative inventory: every block and item of the game and of the loaded mods, sorted into tabs (blocks, building parts, tools and armour, food, other items) with a search that finds names in your language, English names and ids like `oak_planks`. A left click takes a full stack, a right click one item, a shift-click puts a full stack straight into the inventory; click a slot to put it there. The bin deletes a stack. **Crafting and armour** leads to the usual inventory with the crafting grid and the armour slots.

**Rules in creative mode**

- Blocks break at once (even bedrock) and drop nothing; chests and furnaces still lose their contents.
- Placing a block keeps your stack, a poured bucket stays full.
- No damage except `/kill`; creatures leave you alone; food stays as it is.
- **Middle mouse** or **B** puts the block you look at into your hand; in survival this selects it when it is in your inventory.

**Changing between the modes**

Survival and creative each have their own inventory and armour. Changing the mode puts the current ones aside and brings back those of the other mode, so creative items never end up in your survival inventory; nothing is lost, and a message in the chat says which inventory you now have. A backpack open in your hand closes. Health and food stay as they were. Chests belong to the world: what you put into a chest in creative mode is there in survival too.

**See also**

[Crafting table](blocks.md#block-qtcraft-crafting_table)

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-spectator"></a>Spectator mode

**Flying through everything**

In **spectator** mode you look around without taking part: you always fly and pass through blocks, water and lava. **Space** rises, **Shift** sinks and **Ctrl** flies faster, like creative flight (10.9 m/s). The mouse wheel sets the flight speed from a tenth to four times the normal one; the hint at the bottom of the screen shows it. Nothing hurts you, not even the void below the world, and you never get hungry.

**Unseen and untouchable**

Other players do not see you, and creatures neither attack you nor run from you. You cannot break, place or use blocks, attack, trade, open chests or your inventory, and you pick nothing up; pressure plates, projectiles and minecarts pass you by. There is no hotbar, health or food on the screen and no hand in the view, only the crosshair. The player list still names you, greyed at the end of the list. The world loads around you as around any player.

**Changing to and from spectator**

Operators change the mode with `/gamemode spectator` (short `sp`, also for another player: `/gamemode spectator Alice`) and back with `/gamemode survival`, `/gamemode creative` or `/gamemode adventure`; a new world can start in it (**Game mode** in the main menu). Spectator keeps your survival inventory and armour as they are. If you leave spectator mode inside blocks, you come out on top of them, at the nearest free place straight above you.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-adventure"></a>Adventure mode

**Playing a prepared world**

**Adventure** is survival for worlds that someone built for you to play: health, hunger, creatures, crafting, eating and fighting work as in survival, and you still open doors, chests, furnaces and crafting tables, switch levers and buttons and trade with villagers. What you cannot do is change the world: blocks do not break when you mine them, and items do nothing to blocks (no placing, tilling, pouring, lighting fire, armour stands or minecarts) unless the world allows it. Armour stands withstand your hits. A short notice says when something is not allowed. Operators switch with `/gamemode adventure` (or `a`), and a new world can start in adventure (**Game mode** in the main menu). Adventure shares its inventory with survival.

**Where breaking and placing are allowed**

The world keeps two allow-lists, which operators change with `/adventure`:
- `/adventure canBreak <item> <block> …`: that item may break those blocks, e.g. `/adventure canBreak iron_pickaxe gold_ore`. Mining then takes as long as in survival and drops what it drops.
- `/adventure canPlaceOn <item> <block> …`: that item may be placed on (or used on) those blocks, e.g. `/adventure canPlaceOn oak_planks stone_bricks`.
- `*` instead of an item stands for every item and the empty hand: `/adventure canBreak * glass`.
- `/adventure` lists the allow-lists, `/adventure remove canBreak <item> [block …]` takes entries away, `/adventure clear` empties both.
The lists belong to the world and apply to every player in adventure mode. Explosions, pistons, liquids, creatures and commands still change blocks as usual; only the players are held back. A glass bottle fills everywhere, since it changes no block.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-rules"></a>Game rules

**Game rules**

**keepInventory**: keep the inventory on death (chosen when creating a world). **daylightCycle**: the time of day runs. **mobSpawning**: creatures appear by themselves. **randomTicks**: plants, crops and farmland change by themselves (off in the showcase world). **quasiConnectivity**: pistons also react to power at the block above them, but only after a block update next to them (on by default). **weatherCycle**: the weather changes by itself (off in the showcase world). **mobGriefing**: creatures change blocks (shadewalkers take and place blocks, silverfish hide in stone, farmers harvest, villagers pick up food). Creatures a structure confines (the showcase world's pens and village rows) and creatures without a mind (`/mob … noai`) never do, whatever the rule says. **explosionBlockDamage**: explosions break blocks and light other explosives; off, they still hurt and push but leave every block as it is (see Explosives). **maxEntityCramming** (a number, 24 by default, like the reference): creatures in one block cell beyond this many take damage until no more than that are left, and drop their loot; 0 switches it off. Mob farms use it to kill what drops into a cell of one block. **playerInvulnerabilityTicks** and **mobInvulnerabilityTicks** (numbers, 10 ticks by default, at most 200): how many game ticks a player or a creature cannot be hurt after a hit; a later hit in that time that is stronger than the strongest so far deals only the difference; 0 makes every hit count. Starving is a hit like any other; lava, fire and magma keep their own rhythm whatever the rules, one hit every 0.5 s. Show a rule with `/gamerule <rule>`; operators change it with `/gamerule <rule> true|false`. The chapter Commands lists every command.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-commands"></a>Commands

**Using commands**

Open the chat with T (or /) and type a command starting with `/`. `/help` lists the commands you may use, `/help <command>` shows how to use one. `~` stands for your position in coordinates (`~2` is two blocks further). A server without a window (`qtcraft-headless --console`) reads the same commands from its console, there without the leading `/`; every command is written to the server log with who ran it.

**Operators and confirmations**

Most commands are for **operators**. In single player you are always an operator; on a server, operators make others operators with `/op <player>` and take the rights away with `/deop`. Every player may use `/help`, `/list`, `/locatebiome`, reading a game rule, `/confirm` and `/cancel`. Commands that destroy a lot wait for `/confirm` (within 30 s; `/cancel` drops them): `/regenerate`, `/fill` and `/clear` of more than 4096 blocks, `/remove` of more than 16 entities, of armour stands or in the whole world.

**Players**

- `/list`: who is online
- `/locatebiome <biome>`: where the nearest place of a biome is
- `/locate village`: where the nearest village is (operators); `/village [<x> <z>]`: its state as JSON, with beds and workstations
- `/kick <player> [reason]`: disconnect a player (he may come back)
- `/ban <player> [reason]`, `/unban <player>`, `/banlist`: keep a name out of this world
- `/whitelist on|off|list|add <player>|remove <player>`: with the whitelist on, only listed players and operators join; a ban holds for operators too
- `/op [player]`, `/deop <player>`: operator rights; `/op` alone lists the operators
- `/tp <x> <y> <z>`, `/tp <player>`, `/tp <player> <x> <y> <z>`, `/tp <player> <target player>`: teleport
- `/say <message>`: a chat line to everyone as the server
Operators, bans and the whitelist are saved with the world. The host of a world cannot be kicked, banned or lose his rights.

**Repairing and cleaning up**

- `/regenerate [<x> <z> [<x2> <z2>]]`: generate the chunk at your feet (or the chunks of an area, at most 64) again as the world type makes it; every change in it is lost, chests and furnaces in it lose their contents
- `/remove <mobs|hostile|animals|ambient|items|stands|all|<creature>|item:<item>> [radius|world]`: remove entities within 32 m (at most 256 m) or in the whole loaded world; `all` means creatures and items
- `/fill <x1> <y1> <z1> <x2> <y2> <z2> <block> [replace <block>]` and `/clear <x1> <y1> <z1> <x2> <y2> <z2> [block]`: fill a box or turn it into air, at most 32768 blocks; `replace` and the block of `/clear` limit the change to one kind of block
- `/setblock <x> <y> <z> <block>`
- `/save`: save now; `/backup [label]`: save and copy the world folder into the folder backups next to the worlds, named with date and time
- `/worldinfo`: loaded chunks, entities, tick times and memory
- `/limits`: the server's limits with their ranges; `/limits set <key> <value>` changes one at once, `/limits reset <key>` sets it back to its default (a dedicated server writes the change into server.json, a single-player world keeps it in world.json with the next save)

**Time and ticks**

- `/time [set day|night|<seconds>]`, `/time add <seconds>`
- `/gamerule <keepInventory|daylightCycle|mobSpawning|randomTicks|fireSpread|quasiConnectivity|weatherCycle|mobGriefing|explosionBlockDamage> [true|false]`; `/gamerule spawnChunkRadius [0-16]`: the chunks around the spawn that stay loaded (0 none, 1 the spawn chunk, 2 the 3 × 3 around it …); `/gamerule maxEntityCramming [0-4096]`: creatures in one block cell beyond which they take damage (0 never); `/gamerule playerInvulnerabilityTicks [0-200]` and `/gamerule mobInvulnerabilityTicks [0-200]`: the game ticks without damage after a hit (0 none)
- `/weather [clear|rain|thunder] [seconds]`: shows the weather; operators set it, for that many seconds or as long as it would last
- `/gamemode [survival|creative|adventure|spectator] [player]`: shows or changes the game mode, `/defaultgamemode [mode]`: the mode of new players
- `/adventure`: shows or changes what players in adventure mode may break and place on (see Adventure mode)
- `/tick freeze` and `/tick unfreeze`: the world stands still (time of day, creatures, liquids, plants, machines, hunger), players keep moving; `/tick step [ticks]` moves a frozen world on
- `/tick rate <1..100>` or `/tick rate reset` (20 per second): how fast the world runs
- `/tick sprint <ticks|seconds s|minutes min|hours h|days d>`: run that much game time as fast as possible (`/tick sprint stop` ends it)
- `/tick query`: rate, measured rate, freezing and the time per tick phase
Rate and freezing are not saved; a world always opens normally.
- `/forceload add <x> <z> [<x2> <z2>]`, `/forceload remove <x> <z> [<x2> <z2>]|all`, `/forceload query [<x> <z>]`: keeps chunks loaded although no player is near them (block coordinates of a chunk or of the corners of an area, at most 256 chunks at once and `forcedChunks` in the world), lets them go or lists them; circuits connected to a forced chunk stay loaded with it

**Items, creatures and armour**

- `/give <item> [count]`, `/spawnitem <item> [count]`
- `/summon <creature> [count] [noai]` (at most 64 awake summoned creatures, server limit `summonLimit`; creatures of the world do not count), `/kill`, `/heal`, `/feed [points]`
- `/mob <nearest|looking|id> noai <on|off>` takes a creature's mind away or gives it back, like `noai` in `/summon`: without a mind it neither walks, swims, attacks nor teleports and changes no block, but it still falls, is pushed and takes damage; it stays with the world.
- `/equip <set|armour|clear>` puts on armour (`/equip iron`)
- `/summon armor_stand [property=value …]` and `/armorstand <nearest|looking|id> <property> [values]` place and change armour stands. Properties: `pose <head|body|left_arm|right_arm|left_leg|right_leg> <x> <y> <z>` (degrees), `rotation <degrees>`, `arms`, `plate`, `small`, `showname` with `true|false`, `name [text]`, `equip <set|item>`, `cape <cape|none>`, `clear`, `reset`; `info` lists them. Stand ids are only valid until the world is loaded again; scripts use `nearest` or `looking`. In `/summon` values follow `=` separated by commas: `/summon armor_stand equip=iron pose=right_arm,90,0,0 small`.

**Commands from mods**

Mods add commands as macros (`commands/*.json`): a name and command lines that run one after another with your rights, so a macro never lets you do more than you may do anyway. `/help` lists them with the others.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-multiplayer"></a>Multiplayer

**Joining a server**

Choose **Multiplayer** in the main menu, type the server's address and your player name and click **Join**. An address is `host`, `host:port`, `192.168.1.20:25601` or `[IPv6 address]:port`; without a port the game uses 25600. Give the password only if the server has one. While the game connects and logs in you can cancel; if the server refuses you, the screen says why (wrong password, full server, other mods, a name that belongs to someone else, a ban). Servers you joined appear under **Recent servers**: a click puts the address into the field, **Join** joins at once, **×** removes the entry. Passwords are never saved.

**Opening your world to the LAN**

In a single-player world, open the menu (Esc) and choose **Open to LAN**. Pick a port (0 takes any free one), an optional password and how many players may join, then **Open to other players**. The screen shows the port and the addresses of your computer; friends in the same network join with one of them. The world is offered over IPv4 only, which your router keeps inside your network; **Also over IPv6** adds IPv6 addresses, which are often reachable from the internet, so set a password then. You keep playing as before and stay the world's operator; **Stop hosting** or leaving the world closes it again. The firewall may ask once whether QtCraft may accept incoming connections.

**Dedicated server and the internet**

A server without a window runs with `qtcraft-headless --server`; its settings (name, message of the day, port, password, player limit, world, view distance, operators) are in `server.json` in its data folder, written at the first start. Players over the internet need the server's port forwarded in the router to the server computer, or a server with a public address; QtCraft uses no relay servers.

**Other players**

Hold **Tab** to see who is online: the server's name and message of the day, every player with operators marked and your own name highlighted. The chat tells when somebody joins or leaves. If the connection ends, the game says why; **Join again** connects to the same server, **Back to menu** leaves. In the menu (Esc) of a network game, **Disconnect** leaves the server; the server keeps your inventory and position.

**Limits of the security**

There are no accounts: every installation has a random key that proves your name on each server (the first player to use a name on a server keeps it). Every connection is encrypted. Each server has its own certificate, which your game remembers at the first join; if a server later shows another one, the game refuses to connect, because someone may be listening in. Choose **Forget the old certificate** only when the server's owner tells you he made a new one. Open a server to the internet only with a password and to people you know.

### <a id="chapter-qtcraft-texture_packs"></a>Texture packs

**Choosing a look**

**Settings → Texture packs** changes how blocks, items, creatures, your character and the icons look. **QtCraft HD** paints the game's own textures at 32, 64 or 128 pixels with finer detail; **Storybook** is a small pack of drawn images with outlines and warm colours. Changes apply at once, also in a running world, and only on your computer: other players keep their own choice.

**Settings → General → Texture filtering** keeps distant ground seen at a flat angle sharp and free of stripes and rings: *4×* by default, *8×* a little sharper still, *2×* and *Off* cost the graphics card less (*Off* shows rings on far grass).

**Several packs at once**

Switch on up to 16 packs. A pack you switch on goes to the top; the arrows change the order. For every texture the highest pack that has it wins, then the next one, and finally the game's own texture, so a small pack can change just a few blocks.

**Your own packs**

A pack is a folder with a **texturepack.json** and PNG images (or texture descriptions) in **textures/qtcraft/**, one per texture, all of the same size from 16 to 512 pixels. **Open texture pack folder** in the settings shows where they go; **Reload** reads them again after a change. Mods can bring packs of their own.

**When something is wrong**

A damaged image or one of the wrong size is skipped and listed under its pack; that texture then comes from the next pack. Very large packs are shown at a lower resolution when the graphics card or the memory would not take them, and the settings say so.
